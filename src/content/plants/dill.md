Aromatic and versatile, dill is a true workhorse in the garden and kitchen. Its feathery, thread-like leaves — known as dill weed — carry a sweet, slightly tangy flavor reminiscent of anise, while the seeds offer a more pungent, slightly bitter punch beloved in pickling brines, breads, and spice blends. Plants grow quickly from seed directly sown in well-drained soil, but resist transplanting due to their deep taproot. Successive sowings every 2–4 weeks ensure a continuous harvest from late spring through summer.

As plants mature they send up tall, hollow stems topped with broad golden umbels that attract an impressive array of beneficial insects — including bees, hoverflies, parasitic wasps, and, most notably, black swallowtail butterfly caterpillars, which feed exclusively on members of the carrot family (Apiaceae). Dill also acts as a natural pest repellent near brassicas, making it an excellent companion for broccoli, cabbage, and cucumbers. To maximize the leaf harvest, pinch off flower buds before they open. If you do let some flower and set seed, dill readily self-sows, often returning the following season without replanting.
