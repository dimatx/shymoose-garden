*Acer palmatum* 'Inaba-shidare' is a laceleaf (dissectum) Japanese maple prized for combining vigor with grace. Where many cutleaf maples stay small and shrubby, 'Inaba-shidare' grows more strongly upright before its branches arch over into a broad, cascading mound — reaching 4–6 feet in its first ten years and eventually 10 feet tall and 12 feet or more across. It is native (as a species) to Japan, Korea, and China, and has been cultivated as a garden treasure for the better part of a century.

## Foliage that holds its color

The cultivar's signature is its deeply dissected, seven-lobed leaves, cut almost to the base into a feathery, thread-like texture. New foliage emerges deep purple in spring, matures to a rich purple-red through summer — resisting the fade to green that afflicts many red laceleaf maples in the heat — and finally ignites to a brilliant red in autumn. Small reddish flowers appear in spring, followed by paired winged samaras, but the plant is grown overwhelmingly for its leaves and its silhouette.

## A specimen for the discerning garden

Its cascading, mushroom-like form makes 'Inaba-shidare' a natural focal point: set it as a specimen near a patio, at the edge of a border, in a rock garden, or beside a pond where the weeping branches can be reflected in the water. It is also an excellent sun-dappled understory plant beneath taller trees, and smaller plants do well in containers. Deer generally leave it alone. The cultivar has earned the RHS Award of Garden Merit for its reliable garden performance.

## Care tips

Plant in moist, organically rich, slightly acidic, well-drained soil, and mulch generously to keep the shallow roots cool and evenly moist. Full sun brings out the strongest color in cooler climates, but in hot-summer regions give it afternoon shade and shelter from drying winds to prevent leaf scorch. Keep pruning to a minimum; if you must prune, do it from late fall to mid-winter while the tree is dormant — pruning in spring or summer causes significant sap bleeding. Watch for aphids, scale, and mites, and avoid high-pH soils, which can cause chlorosis.
