Northern bayberry is a dense, rounded, deciduous-to-semi-evergreen shrub
native to sand dunes, coastal thickets, and woodland edges from Newfoundland
south to North Carolina. It's an exceptionally tough, low-fuss shrub — the
kind of plant that thrives on neglect in the very conditions (poor sand,
salt spray, wind) that would stress most garden shrubs.

## Through the seasons

The grayish-green to dark green leaves are leathery and aromatic, releasing a
spicy, bay-like scent when crushed. Male and female flowers appear on
separate plants as small, non-showy yellowish-green catkins in spring — you
need at least one male nearby for the females to set fruit. By late summer,
pollinated female plants are studded with small, waxy, silvery-gray berries
that persist on the bare branches well into the following spring, making
this a valuable winter food source for birds.

## Care tips

Bayberry is remarkably adaptable: it tolerates drought, wet spots, poor and
sandy soils, road salt, and coastal winds, and it even fixes its own
nitrogen through a symbiotic relationship with soil bacteria. It suckers
readily to form informal colonies, which makes it a great choice for
hedges, screens, mass plantings, or erosion control on a slope — just give
it room to spread, or plan to remove unwanted suckers occasionally.

> **Heads up:** This shrub is dioecious — male and female flowers grow on
> separate plants. If berries (and the classic bayberry-candle wax) are the
> goal, plant at least one male for every few females.
