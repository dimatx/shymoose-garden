*Salix babylonica*, the weeping willow, is one of the most instantly
recognizable trees in the world — a fast-growing deciduous tree whose long,
slender branches and narrow leaves hang in graceful, cascading curtains that can
sweep all the way to the ground. It is among the first trees to leaf out in
spring and among the last to drop its leaves in fall, holding its soft green
(fading to golden yellow) far into the season.

## A tree for water

Weeping willow is happiest with wet feet. It thrives at the edges of ponds and
lakes and in any low spot that holds water, and it is often planted on slopes and
banks where its thirsty, spreading roots help control erosion. That same vigor
comes with a caution: the wood is weak and breaks easily under snow, ice, or
wind, and the aggressive roots can seek out and clog water or sewer lines, so
it's best given room well away from the house and pipes.

## Life around it

Beyond its beauty, the willow is quietly busy: it is a larval host for the
viceroy butterfly, supports a range of specialized native bees, and its early
catkins offer some of the first nectar of the year. Set beside water where its
reflection doubles the effect, few trees are as evocative.
